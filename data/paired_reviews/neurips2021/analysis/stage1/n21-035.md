**Verdict.** A accepted on 6, 7, 6, 6; B rejected on 5, 5, 5. This is not a weighting difference. Each committee decided on an issue the other never heard. B tested the paper's motivating claim that polar sectors avoid the memory and compute that cartesian rectangles waste. The paper does not support it. Figure 1 puts PolarStream within about 2 ms of the cartesian Han et al. at every sector count. No memory figure appears. Polar detection also trails cartesian pillars at full sweep (51.2 vs 52.3 mAP) and at 4 sectors (53.2 vs 54.9). The rebuttal conceded no runtime gain on a V100 and said "Our major contribution is not polar representation". The paper lists that representation as its first contribution. B's AC rejected on this point. No A reviewer raised it, and Reviewer_ijm6 repeated the efficiency claim as fact. A's doubts were the reimplemented baselines and the gap to full-sweep methods. The rebuttal recast the baselines as a contribution: a promised open streaming benchmark with code. That pledge moved Reviewer_88yB above threshold and appears in A's meta-review. No B reviewer asked about baselines, so B never heard the pledge. Both committees credited the gains over streaming baselines in Table 1.

**Committee A (poster).** Reviewer_2ez1 (1.5 hours) wrote a specific, positive review. Reviewer_8rdp gave 7 at confidence 5 in a two-sentence review. It never joins the discussion and is no evidence of quality. Without it, A still averages 6.0. Reviewer_88yB and Reviewer_ijm6, both at confidence 3, raised the real doubts: unverified reimplementations, incremental novelty, and accuracy below full-sweep methods. The authors answered with a heavier-backbone table that matches CenterPoint and Cylinder3D on val, and with the release pledge. ijm6 called the release a "good citizenship" bonus. 88yB cited that phrase and moved from below threshold to 6. The AC's two sentences cite "4 expert reviewers" and the promised benchmark. Two of them chose confidence 3, which says details were not carefully checked.

**Committee B (reject).** Each B reviewer spent 4 hours, at confidence 4 or 5. Reviewer_dNFe called the paper "well-written" and "technically sound". It still led with the polar justification, citing Table 2 and Figure 1. Figure 1 bears this out. Table 2 is weaker evidence, because it compares equal full-sweep inputs. Reviewer_X4zp made the same point and added clarity, missing visualizations and an underspecified stratified convolution. The main text never states the number of strata. Reviewer_1vpo rated on novelty and readability. After the rebuttal it adopted X4zp's polar concern. It also said all reviewers flagged readability, which dNFe did not. The authors' feature-map tables showed savings only at 8 or more sectors. Their embedded-hardware argument had no measurement. All three kept 5, and the AC concurred.

**Issues one committee raised and the other did not.** Only B checked the efficiency claim and the polar detection deficit (Reviewer_dNFe, Reviewer_X4zp). Only B asked for visualizations, a single-frame baseline and undistortion in the backbone; the rebuttal supplied all three. Only B argued that detection matters more than segmentation (Reviewer_dNFe). That view discounts polar's clearest gains, in mIoU and PQ at every sector count. Both sides named uncited warping work, but only B counted it against novelty (Reviewer_1vpo). Only A questioned the reimplemented baselines, which drew the release pledge. Only A asked about data-loading time (Reviewer_2ez1) and moving objects in bidirectional padding (Reviewer_88yB). The authors skipped the second question. Reviewer_88yB said the paper does not relate itself to other cylindrical methods; Section 2.1 does, briefly. Neither committee asked whether context padding works on cartesian sectors. The authors assert it needs polar coordinates but never test this.
