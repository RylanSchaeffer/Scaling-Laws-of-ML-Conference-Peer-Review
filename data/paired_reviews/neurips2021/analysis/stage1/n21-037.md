**Verdict**

Both committees recommended a poster, for the same reasons. Every reviewer found the core result interesting. A model built on ImageNet-pretrained VGG16, with eccentricity-dependent pooling and no training on the task, reproduces the polarity of five of six classical search asymmetries. Training on 90-degree-rotated images reverses one of them. Both committees saw the same weaknesses: the fit is qualitative only, the model reverses Experiment 5, the training-diet test covers only Experiment 2, and the search-asymmetry literature is thinly cited. Both treated these as revision items. In A the rebuttal promised new analyses; in B it also showed some. Both raised scores. Final means were 7.0 (7, 6, 8) and 6.75 (7, 7, 5, 8). Neither committee had a 9 or a case for a spotlight; both meta-reviews restate the score consensus. The one real difference is B's detractor, Reviewer_CMyL, whose specific objections check out against the paper and were never resolved. B accepted over him, so the agreement does not depend on A lacking such a reviewer.

**Committees**

Committee A (7, 6, 8). Reviewer_cDnL wrote the sharpest review, and each point is correct. Eq. 2 convolves layer l+1 target features over layer l search features and never says the former pool the latter. The target features carry a needless fixation index. Line 105 says VGG16 uses average pooling. The MNIST and rotated models, trained from scratch, have no from-scratch ImageNet control. The authors conceded all four. Reviewer_iR66 rose from below threshold to 6, explicitly on trust: "Given all the promises made in the detailed reply". Reviewer_U5qd gave 8 at confidence 5 in three short paragraphs. Its one criticism, that the training-diet test covers a single domain, is correct. The AC's four sentences count votes.

Committee B (7, 7, 5, 8). The rebuttal moved three reviewers. Reviewer_F9R3 rose to 7 for the added intuition about eccentricity and top-down modulation. Reviewer_CJM4 rose to 7 for the motivation argument and "promised extensive experiments". Reviewer_CMyL rose slightly, to 5, for the scanpath comparison and fitted reaction times. Reviewer_h9eJ's 8 is detailed and accurate. It notes that in Figure 5B the architecture matters more than the training set: the ablations score about 0.05 and 0.084, MNIST training about 0.19. Yet the submission's last sentence credits training statistics "purely". CJM4 read the same figure backwards and missed that IVSN, a VGG16 network, scores about 0.32. CMyL (confidence 5, 11 hours) is right on three points. The weighting at l.139-143 rescales each map by max(A)/(max(A)-min(A)); when a map's minimum is zero, this restores the raw map and cancels the normalization. The "top-down modulation" maps only pick the next fixation; they match the target's pooled features against the search image one layer below. Figure 3B's model curves do not match the macaque curves its caption says they match. He was wrong that the paper omits limitations. The authors' second reply came four days before the decision and drew no answer. The AC listed his concerns as clarifications.

**Issues raised by one committee only**

Only A raised the missing from-scratch ImageNet control, the untested claim that the pooling layers "can be easily integrated", and the average-pooling error (cDnL). Only A raised strawman baselines, alternatives to winner-take-all, and untested asymmetries such as colour (iR66). Only B raised the self-cancelling weighting, the "top-down" label and the receptive-field fit (CMyL). Only B asked for a scanpath comparison with human eye movements (F9R3, CMyL, h9eJ). Only B noted that rotation changes more than lighting (F9R3), questioned the motivation (F9R3, CJM4, AC), disputed calling 66.7% top-1 accuracy "similar" to 72% (CJM4), and noted missing human error bars (h9eJ). None changed a decision.
