**Verdict.** The committees saw the same gaps and weighted them differently. The paper compares only with OP3. It has no downstream or planning task. Table 1 reports the best of three OAT runs, with no variance. Re-appearance rests on single qualitative examples (Figures 6 and 7). In A, the two most careful reviewers, Reviewer_ayAi (confidence 5) and Reviewer_ApBk, held these gaps against it. A's only champion, Reviewer_sjbW (8), spent two hours and listed one minor con; the AC gave that score no visible weight. B drew three satisfied reviewers (8, 7, 7). B also saw evidence A never saw. The authors posted a robot-stacking RL result only in B's forum, and Reviewer_gBUs then raised the score to 6. In A, ayAi was promised RL results "in the next few days"; none arrived. The authors answered A's two critics on 2021-08-25, 15 days after the other A answers. They told ApBk that a missing baseline "is not grounds for rejection". Neither critic replied. A's AC then cited a "lack of demonstration of object permanence". That overstates the reviews: ApBk listed the shown re-appearance as a strength and asked only for recognition at encoding steps. No one found an error in a key result.

**Committee A (reject).** Ratings 6, 8, 5, 4; without sjbW the mean is 5.0. sjbW sells the paper as improving out-of-distribution generalisation of RL agents, which it never tests. ayAi judged the contribution small because only AlignNet is new. But ayAi called the transition model "a simple LSTM"; it is a transformer followed by a SlotLSTM (lines 133-136). ApBk's checkable points hold. ARI scores each frame's segmentation, so no number measures slot consistency over time. No curve shows error against rollout length. SCALOR is not cited. The memory has 12 slots, while Playroom rooms hold 10 to 45 objects. Lines 128-129 show no memory update during the unroll. The rebuttal said alignment cannot be measured in an unsupervised model. Yet the paper already uses ground-truth masks, which give each object's identity (Section 4.2). Reviewer_NjnN kept 6 and added a wish for SCALOR. The AC followed the critics.

**Committee B (poster).** Ratings 7, 6, 6, 8, 7, 5. Reviewer_Jy59's 7 is a two-hour review by a self-declared non-expert. Reviewer_sozU gave 8, though its own text says the ARI numbers do not match the rollouts. Reviewer_vmwV (7) doubted scalability but still accepted. Reviewer_eFBe (5) read the work as segmentation "from third person views". It also objected to appendices after the checklist, which NeurIPS 2021 allowed. eFBe did not reply to the rebuttal. gBUs tied the score to any downstream task. The stacking result is thin: three seeds, one near parity, harder tasks mid-training. Reviewer_V6Rr later wrote that most concerns were resolved. The AC wrote "Reviewers agreed", which ignores eFBe's 5.

**Issues one committee raised and the other did not.** Only A asked for a temporal-consistency metric, a rollout-length curve, a SCALOR baseline, and recognition of objects that re-appear during encoding. Only A called the contribution too small for NeurIPS. Only B asked for seed variance; checklist item 3(c) confirms Table 1 shows the best run per model. Only B noted that the background dominates pixel error, that the figures are too small, and that the C-SWM claim is untested. Only B said SCOFF and NPS contradict the abstract's novelty claim. One A doubt is misplaced: ayAi asked how the model predicts Figure 5's red object. The caption calls it the avatar's base, which appears when the agent looks down; the action carries that. The authors instead pointed to frames t = 0 to 2, which show no base.
