**Verdict**

Both committees recommended a poster for the same reasons, on matching score profiles: A 5, 6, 6, 7 (mean 6.0) and B 7, 7, 6, 5 (mean 6.25). Each saw a clean theory result on a basic problem: a convex loss that avoids the partition function, with consistency, asymptotic normality and polynomial finite-sample bounds. Each treated the weaknesses as revisions. In each committee, one reviewer rose to 6 after the rebuttal (sAxR, xSou), and one 5 stayed (kMiC, nf2S). The committees found different weaknesses, and the submission confirms most of them. A pressed the statistics: (k1k2)^2/α^4 samples, exp(r^T d) in Lemma 3.1, and "≈" inside theorems. B pressed computation and prior art: the gradient needs a centering integral that the paper never costs, and the loss extends an uncited 2016 objective. No one argued for more: the top ratings are 7s, and B's two 7s are thin.

**Committees**

Committee A (5, 6, 6, 7). Reviewer_SSuU gave the best-argued 7: 12 hours, specific questions, and a follow-up on 2021-08-31. It asked whether the cited hardness results survive Assumption 4.1. The authors answered by example, not by proof. The same review lists "asymptotic efficiency" as a strength, but the paper disclaims it (l.86, l.367-368). Reviewer_kMiC's 5 is principled. Lemma 3.1 carries exp(r^T d), Theorem 4.3 uses "≈", and the paper never discusses the 1/α^4 rate. The authors conceded that matrices suffice and that r and d must be O(log k1k2). kMiC never replied. Reviewer_sAxR wanted experiments but rose to 6: the theory was "good enough to make up for" their absence. Reviewer_kDGo gave a fair, generic 6. The AC called the paper borderline and accepted on its own reading: "a new idea that can inspire other researchers". One AC premise is wrong. The optimization does use sparsity and low-rank constraints (Table 1); only the bound ignores them.

Committee B (7, 7, 6, 5). The two 7s are thin. Reviewer_VbcL's main review is two sentences, at confidence 3. Reviewer_xzAW spent 2 hours. Its one specific point, the uncited Vuffray et al. 2016, is right. Neither replied to the rebuttal. Reviewer_xSou wrote the most technical review: polynomial rather than logarithmic dependence on dimension, a loss close to ISO, and an undescribed centering step. xSou raised the score after the authors argued that logarithmic rates exist only for node-wise-sparse models. Reviewer_nf2S's 5 arrived on 2021-09-02, after the discussion. It asked whether the gradient stays polynomial in p. The submission never costs the centering E_U[Φ] (Definition 2.1), and Theorem 4.3 charges only k1k2n per gradient. The AC's meta-review rests on this point alone, and overstates it. X is a box or a ball in every example, so uniform Monte Carlo approximates the centering for any bounded Φ. Only the error analysis is missing. The camera-ready concedes the point.

**Issues raised by one committee only**

Only A raised the 1/α^4 rate and lower bounds (kMiC, SSuU, kDGo), the exponential dependence on r and d (kMiC), and "≈" in theorems (kMiC). Only A asked whether MLE stays hard under Assumption 4.1, over which distribution its expectation runs, and for worked examples such as truncated Gaussians (SSuU). Only B raised the gradient cost and the dependence on p (nf2S, xSou, AC). Only B placed the loss next to ISO and GISO (xzAW, xSou) and listed missing prior work: Vuffray et al. 2016, Dagan et al. 2021, Klivans and Meka 2017. The novelty split is the sharpest. A's AC called the loss "a new idea"; B's reviewers called it an extension of ISO, and l.274-275 credit [51, 40]. Both committees asked for experiments and doubted the tensor notation.
