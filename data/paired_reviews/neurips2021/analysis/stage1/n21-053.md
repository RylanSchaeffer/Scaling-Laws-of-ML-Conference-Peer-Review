**Verdict.** The committees weighted the same facts differently. Most reviewers praised the model, and six of eight, including B's top scorer, doubted the evidence. The evidence is one synthetic test on data sampled from an RBN and one study of 24 major Bach preludes. The paper compares one prelude with an expert analysis, and its own caption says the model misses the large-scale grouping. Committee A made this thin evidence decisive: 4, 4, 4, 5. Committee B's three positive reviewers valued the formalism and either praised the experiments uncritically or excused them: 8, 7, 6, with one 5. Reviewer_1oXa gave 8 yet wrote "I have yet to be convinced of important real-world applications". A's reviews were more searching: three run 530 to 1,578 words, and two examine the music figures; B's four run 312 to 396 words. Nothing in B's record argues for a spotlight. Its AC shared the detractor's overclaiming concern and concluded only that the work "may be enough for justifying publication".

**Committee A (reject).** Reviewer_c2bx (7 hours) asked for related work on hidden tree Markov models and latent tree learning, a test of the single-Gaussian approximation, and benchmarks beyond music. After the response it raised "a bit", to 4, and stayed unconvinced. Reviewer_dyhB found a gap in the Chomsky-normal-form conversion, which the authors conceded. It noted that the Bach tree looks "wildly different" from the expert tree; the authors agreed the upper levels "have almost nothing in common". Reviewer_uEGL kept 5 and asked for a revised paper with comparisons on independent datasets. Reviewer_k7kf (confidence 1, one hour, 133 words) calls the paper a "Good contribution" yet rates 4. The AC cited the scores, then gave its own reason: PCFG-style Bayesian networks are "an old issue", and continuous graphical models have "a very large literature". The reviewers had objected mainly to the evaluation, and the AC named no prior work.

**Committee B (spotlight).** No review was edited after posting, so the rebuttal moved nothing. Reviewer_gxvj (7) cited "quantitative improvements" over change point detection as significance. It never notes that the test data come from an RBN. Reviewer_1oXa (8) praised the writing and the inference but wrote that "some much simpler method could also have done this". Its 8 sits above its own text. Reviewer_VzMU (6) wrote that the music results "demonstrate the effectiveness" and that limitations "are discussed". The paper has no limitations section, no music baseline, and a caption that reports failure. Reviewer_K4dB (5) argued three times that the general RBN claimed is far from the Gaussian case delivered. The paper confirms this: both experiments use Gaussian RBNs, and the submitted abstract and Figure 1 present RBNs as continuous models. The AC agreed with K4dB, then accepted on the positive majority.

**Issues one committee raised and the other did not.** Only A asked for related work on hidden tree Markov models, latent tree learning and factor graph grammars; the camera-ready adds all three. Only A found the normal-form gap, asked to measure the error of the single-Gaussian approximation, and asked why only identity (78%) and fifth (22%) transpositions get weight. Only A learned that the authors made the expert annotation themselves. Only B raised sum-product networks, Monte Carlo inference and the name "continuous PCFG". Neither committee named Gaussian mixture latent vector grammars (ACL 2018), a grammar with continuous latent states and approximate Gaussian parsing. A public comment after acceptance did, and the first author replied that "the Gaussian case seems to be closely related indeed". This supports A's novelty doubt more than B's hope that RBNs become "an important model".
