**Verdict.** Both committees saw the same core facts and weighted them differently. Then B's area chair (AC) broke a 6, 6, 5, 5 split toward acceptance. The core fact: iSPN learns each interventional distribution from samples of that intervention, with the mutilated graph given (Prop. 1; lines 218–220, 225–227). It never infers an interventional distribution from observational data. In A, two reviewers (ratings 3 and 2, confidence 4 and 3) made this the reason to reject. The paper confirms their reading. In B, Reviewer_4XqK saw the same fact but treated it as a question of scope. The rebuttal evaded the point in A, and no A reviewer replied. In B, two reviewers rose after the rebuttal, to a final 6, 5, 6, 5. B's AC accepted "despite the lack of full support", because the work is original and "the authors addressed all the concerns". The record contradicts the second reason.

**Committee A (reject; 7, 3, 5, 2).** Reviewer_aP4W (3) wrote that inference from "data sampled from the same intervention reduces to standard probabilistic inference". It quoted the proof of Prop. 1. Lines 218–220 confirm that the paper assumes "actual interventional data". aP4W also asked what SPN tractability adds when the graph is known. The paper never compares with the causal BN; lines 277–279 exclude it as the "best performing" gold standard. Reviewer_KcVJ (2) made the same point on Prop. 1: with interventional data "the whole problem goes away". KcVJ's "5 pages without introducing anything new" overstates; the background is about one page. Reviewer_3eLf (5) asked for consistency theory; the paper has only Prop. 1. The lone champion, Reviewer_mLY8 (7, confidence 3, 2 hours, details "not carefully checked"), argued potential impact. It called the causal estimates good, which Fig. 5 does not support. The authors called aP4W's tractability question "an advanced question which naturally follows". They told KcVJ its assessment was "widely exaggerated". No review changed. The AC followed the majority: the link between SPNs and causal inference was "not adequately developed".

**Committee B (poster; 6, 5, 6, 5).** Reviewer_4XqK (8 hours) noted that the input data "already contains a target of inference such as P(y | do(x))". It also saw that Prop. 1 holds without Markovianity. So interventional data, not the Markov assumption, set the method's scope. 4XqK still gave 6. Reviewer_s1DW asked whether DoWhy beats iSPN in Fig. 5. It does, in all four rows. In three rows iSPN is even further from the truth than naive conditioning. The authors never answered. Reviewer_WdJo rose to 6 with a one-line note. The reply it accepted centred on the Papantonis-and-Belle paragraph that A's KcVJ also received; WdJo's objection was one that paragraph answers. Reviewer_UWyh rose to 5 on promises (complexity analysis, CausalGAN comparison). It still found the contribution unclear and would not recommend acceptance. The AC reframed the weaknesses as Markovian scope and readability.

**Issues one committee raised and the other did not.** Only A asked what tractability adds over forward sampling in a known graph. Only A questioned universal approximation against tractability, asked for consistency theory, and flagged the unexplained "shared parameters". Only B found DoWhy more accurate than iSPN, and asked about scaling, CausalGAN (not cited) and ADD-based BNs. Both raised the interventional-data setting, the thin SPN background, the late and vague definition, small simulated data sets and hidden confounding. B's AC mentioned neither the interventional-data setting nor Fig. 5. A's AC named the weak link between SPNs and causal inference.
