**Verdict**

Both committees rejected, for overlapping reasons. The paper's own tables set the ceiling. WeaveNet clearly beats the learning baselines at N ≤ 9. Against PowerBalance it wins 8 of 10 settings at N = 20, but only 3 of 10 at N = 30. It fails at N = 100 (pSEq 258 against 49), as l.315-316 concedes. No final rating in either committee is above 6. A (5, 5, 6, 4) rejected by consensus; its only 6 came from a two-hour review that took the method for deep RL. B's reviewers were warmer (6, 4, 6). B's two 6s valued the gain over learning baselines; A weighed PowerBalance and scale. B's lone detractor, Reviewer_cFCG, decided B's outcome. Its critique (gap-to-ideal plots, no error bars, an unclear relation to DBM) moved Reviewer_t5vd from 7 to 6 in internal discussion. B's AC then rejected despite two 6s. Both ACs leaned on the figure critique, which is only half right. On pBal the plotted spread at N = 9 is under 4% of the ideal. On pSEq, DBM-18f's excess cost (about 1.2) is five times WN-18f's (about 0.25), on an ideal of 4.7. Only B found a real technical error: argmax of a symmetric doubly stochastic output need not give a one-to-one matching (l.187-191). The authors conceded it, but no AC mentioned it.

**Committees**

Committee A (mean 5.0). Reviewer_wb66 (4, 10 hours) wrote the most specific review. It said the figures overstate the gains, gains over PowerBalance are often "non-existing or very small", error bars and solver runtimes are missing, and Section 5.1 names no dataset. Each point holds; the figure point only for Bal. Reviewer_PXDn (5, confidence 5) asked why the architecture works and where it fails. Its follow-up cited graduated-assignment work; the authors called this out of scope. Reviewer_EJfv (5) raised N ≠ M fairness and the UD and N = 100 weaknesses. After the rebuttal it kept 5: results "not very strong against hand-crafted algorithms". Reviewer_nn6T (6, 294 words) wrote that the problem "is solved by deep reinforcement learning", but training is unsupervised (l.172-175). It ignored the correction. No score moved. The AC cited scalability and "misleading figures" in two sentences.

Committee B (mean 5.33). Reviewer_t5vd first gave 7 and found the l.187 error with a valid counterexample. After internal discussion it adopted cFCG's figure and error-bar points and dropped to 6. It also cited the rebuttal's admission of 100 times slower inference. Its key quote is misplaced: l.250 ("obviously worse") describes Fig. 5, where DBM-6's stable rate at N = 9 is about 0.40 against 0.75 and 0.85. Reviewer_cFCG (4, 9 hours) was right on its main points: the 3% pBal spread, no restarts or error bars, an undeclared DBM re-implementation, and collapse under shift (Table 3: 2.8% and 0.1% stable). It was wrong that the problem is first defined on page 3, that no validation set exists, and that WeaveNet is supervised. Reviewer_a437 (6) asked why anyone would prefer WeaveNet to PowerBalance. It had offered to raise its score but kept 6 in one line. The AC cited the DBM comparison and the experiments' presentation.

**Issues raised by one committee only**

Only A raised N ≠ M fairness (EJfv), missing insight into why the method works (PXDn), the two-layer GIN baseline and Section 5.1's unnamed dataset (wb66), and two-matrix inputs (nn6T). Only B raised the false argmax claim (t5vd), the unguaranteed stable one-to-one output (t5vd, a437), weak motivation and misused "end-to-end" (cFCG), and collapse under distribution shift (cFCG). The paper confirms the l.187 error, the unnamed dataset and the collapse. None changed a decision.
