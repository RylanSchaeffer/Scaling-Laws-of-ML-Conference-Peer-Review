**Verdict.** Committee A rejected and Committee B accepted because the committees weighted the overlap with prior work differently, and their chairs drew the line in different places. The evidence was largely the same. A rated the paper 6, 6, 6, 6. B rated it 6, 5, 7, 5 before the rebuttal and 7, 6, 7, 5 after it. In A, Reviewer_BNRH showed that Sections 4.2-4.3 reuse the training scheme of Nash & Durkan's Autoregressive Energy Machines (AEM): energy network, learned mixture proposal, per-dimension importance sampling, joint objective. The submission cites AEM there only for two side points (L170-171, L182). Reviewer_qrbW showed that "the first work to leverage this formulation" (L153-154) ignores Uria et al., whom the paper itself cites (L78-80). Both points are correct, and a rebuttal cannot remove them. A's chair rejected four above-threshold ratings as "right on the border" and called the method "obvious to experts". In B, Reviewer_ENb9 knew that the method is AEM in ACFlow's setting and called it "an interesting combination". B asked for clarifications, the rebuttal answered them, two scores rose, and B's chair counted three accepts against one 5.

**Committee A (reject).** BNRH (confidence 4) wrote the most informed review in either committee, and still rated 6 because the experiments were acceptable. After the rebuttal BNRH replied "My opinion of the paper remains the same" and added that order-agnostic MADE training answers the authors' reason for their design. The novelty point spread: qrbW's update calls the method "a (somewhat trivial) combination of several existing pieces" but keeps the 6. Reviewer_4qhh wanted VAEAC's imputation benchmarks. The rebuttal added three datasets, and 4qhh was "mostly satisfied". Energy ACE beats VAEAC clearly only on Mushroom and is slightly worse on White Wine (0.853 against 0.850). Reviewer_LU53 asked how marginals are computed and asked for marginal results; L110 and Table 2 already give both. The meta-review says reviewers "felt that the experimental validation is not strong". That misstates three of the four reviews: BNRH, LU53 and qrbW praised the experiments.

**Committee B (poster).** B's reviewers were less sure (confidence 3, 3, 4, 3) and asked about mechanics: the number of importance samples, a grid instead of a proposal, non-autoregressive training, a non-negative energy. The authors answered with arguments and one measurement, not experiments. Reviewer_TGax rose from 6 to 7 and ENb9 from 5 to 6. Reviewer_4vM4 (7, two hours) asked why the gains in Figure 2 concentrate at missing rate 0.1. The figure confirms the pattern, and the rebuttal did not explain it. Reviewer_AEG1's 5 contradicts its own "I would be in favor of having the paper presenting". L205-207 answer its question on chained normalizers. Its objection to mean imputation is wrong: each coordinate mean of p(x_u|x_o) equals the mean of the one-dimensional conditional, so ignoring dependence costs nothing. The meta-review repeats this objection as a requested revision and never mentions novelty.

**Issues raised by one committee only.** Only A raised:

- uncredited reuse of the AEM scheme (BNRH);
- the overstated "first work" claim and the missing Query Training citation (qrbW);
- input duplication instead of MADE-style masking (BNRH);
- missing imputation benchmarks: VAEAC's datasets, CelebA, Omniglot, GAN baselines (4qhh);
- a Locally Masked Convolution baseline and a weight-sharing ablation (LU53).

Only B raised a finite normalizer, design complexity (grid, skip inputs, per-feature latent vectors), error accumulation across dimensions, mean imputation, and the 0.1 missing-rate pattern. Both raised importance-sample counts, order dependence at test time, wall-clock cost and dataset breadth. Both saw that ACE builds on AEM; A counted it against the paper, B as a strength.
