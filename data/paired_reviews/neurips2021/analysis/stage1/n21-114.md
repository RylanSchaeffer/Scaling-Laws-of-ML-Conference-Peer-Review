**Verdict.** Committee A rejected and Committee B accepted because the two reviewer pools put different weaknesses at the centre and weighted the one they shared differently. A rated the paper 4, 3, 5, 7; B rated it 7, 7, 7, 5. The rebuttal widened the gap: one A reviewer moved down, three B reviewers moved up. Both committees saw that the conclusions outrun the data. In Figure 4a, failure-state similarity falls in one game, rises in two and stays flat in two. Section 4.3 still concludes that failures are "more likely related to perceptual errors" (L258-260). In A, Reviewer_YjzP (confidence 5, 8 hours) and Reviewer_iyMr made this the core objection, with the unexamined validity of perturbation saliency as "attention". A's chair added an unaddressed performance confound and a missing citation (Atrey et al. 2020, absent from the submission). In B, the main objection was the slowed, semi-frame-by-frame human data. The authors answered it well, and B's chair agreed. Reviewer_sG7Q also raised the overclaiming. The authors gave sG7Q the same paragraph that YjzP rejected, and sG7Q accepted it. The program chairs required removal of the causal claims at the end of 4.3 and 4.4. A's objection was correct; B's judgement that it was fixable prevailed.

**Committee A (reject).** YjzP wrote the decisive review: the paper "picks one of the possible conclusions, presents it as the ground-truth takeaway". The paper confirms this. Sections 4.3 and 4.4 end in causal readings, and the human-attention prior in Section 5 has no experiment. YjzP's follow-up called the rebuttal "speculation that hasn't been directly tested". iyMr (3) asked whether better policies simply make relevant pixels matter more; the paper has no control for this. iyMr also asked for error-analysis statistics that Figure 4a already gives, and never replied to the rebuttal. Reviewer_1Bvp re-read the paper and lowered the score to 5 for lack of "clear conclusions". The only accept, Reviewer_Xxje (7), spent one hour, wrote generic praise and skipped the discussion; the chair says Xxje privately raised concerns. The chair followed the three rejections and leaned on YjzP.

**Committee B (poster).** sG7Q's objection was that slowed play may change human attention. The authors argued that slowed play gives accurate gaze and that agents also act without time pressure. Reviewer_bEDj and sG7Q accepted this, and the chair repeated it. The rebuttal's claim that agents "take an action every 16 frames" is not in the paper, which states NoFrameskip-v4 with Stable Baselines defaults. Nobody checked it, but the chair's argument does not need it. Reviewer_hVFS (7-8 hours) wrote the most detailed review in either committee. hVFS found single-example claims in 4.2 and 4.4 and a Figure 3b caption that the human panel does not support; both are correct. hVFS rose to 7 after the authors defended the L2 policy metric; the requested quantification was only promised. Reviewer_HwvK's 7 (one hour, confidence 3, no discussion) adds little. sG7Q kept a 5, calling the remaining reasons "more subjective than objective".

**Issues raised by one committee only.** Only A raised:

- whether perturbation saliency measures attention (YjzP, iyMr, chair);
- the performance confound in the similarity-score correlation (iyMr, chair);
- the untested human-attention prior (YjzP);
- no test for the learning trend (iyMr; the camera-ready adds r(8) = 0.992).

Only B raised the slowed human data, the L2 policy distance, duplicate FIRE actions, the four-frame input to the human model and the gaze-model data split. Both raised the overclaimed conclusions of 4.3-4.4, the one-correlation "generalization" in Section 4.5, and predicted rather than measured human attention. A counted these against the paper; B treated them as fixable framing.
