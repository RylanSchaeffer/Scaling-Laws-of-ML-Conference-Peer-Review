**Verdict.** Both committees weighted the same facts differently, and the area chairs (ACs) decided the outcome. The scores are almost equal: A gave 6, 6, 5, 7, 5 (mean 5.8) and B gave 6, 5, 6, 7 (mean 6.0). Both sides saw a new identifiability condition that generalises equal variance. Both saw algorithms close to known ones, weak simulations, and no evidence of how often the conditions hold. B's AC adjudicated. It asked the authors to compare the d^2 rate with prior work. It called the main objection, that Condition 2 is too strong, "largely a misunderstanding". The paper confirms this: Theorem 3.1 needs only Condition 1, and Lemma 3.2 makes Condition 2 a special case. The AC called a demand for lower bounds "asking too much" and accepted. A's AC ran no visible discussion. No A reviewer posted a reply; only Reviewer_aYyX edited a review, up to 6. A's meta-review names no error. It calls recovery under the condition "fairly straightforward" and asks for checks on real-world data, which no reviewer requested. It rejects on "mixed" opinions. One of A's two 5s rests partly on a misreading that its author never revisited.

**Committee A (reject).** Reviewer_WuUC's 5 is the most substantive review. It says the gain over Ghoshal and Honorio matters only for sparse graphs. That holds for complete DAGs: with one node per layer, (C1) reduces to the old ordering condition (lines 158-162). WuUC also noted the exponential cost in M and that no experiment breaks Condition 1; line 317 confirms that all data satisfy Condition 2. Reviewer_YmV1's 5 calls the method "heuristic" and says any functional, "say negative H", would work. Both claims are wrong. Theorem 3.1 rests on an entropy inequality, and Algorithm 1 sorts entropies in ascending order. Reviewer_aYyX correctly noted that M <= log d first appears on page 7 (line 264). Reviewer_Wj5d's 7 took one hour at confidence 2 and carries little weight. The authors sent Reviewer_kVE1 the unfaithful example it asked for; kVE1 never replied. The meta-review even calls the condition "strictly weaker" than faithfulness; faithfulness implies only (C2).

**Committee B (poster).** Reviewer_sG1J (confidence 5, two hours) first read Condition 2 as required. After the rebuttal, sG1J accepted the correction and rose to 5. sG1J stayed at "borderline" because of the d^2 rate, the missing lower bound and the presentation. These facts are accurate: Theorem 5.1 needs n of order d^2 r log^3 d, and line 333 leaves lower bounds open. Reviewer_g49G found the unfaithful example "very helpful", kept 6, and called sG1J's concerns "all valid". Reviewer_E5SA's review is generic praise; E5SA later lowered to 6, deferring to the others. Reviewer_Zh6d's 7 rests on a three-sentence main review. It says the method does "as well and sometimes better". At n = 1000 and d >= 40, Figure 2 shows Algorithm 1 with the highest SHD in all twelve cases. B's acceptance rests on the AC, not on its champions.

**Issues one committee raised and the other did not.** Only A asked why entropy and not another functional, and why not start from the PC skeleton. Only A questioned the gain over Ghoshal and Honorio on dense graphs. Only A flagged the late M <= log d assumption, missing run times and the continuous-optimisation literature. Only A's AC asked for real-world data. Only B made the d^2 rate and the missing lower bound a case against the paper. Only B misread Condition 2 as required. Both sides saw a method that is worse than PC and GES at n = 1000 and comparable at larger n.
