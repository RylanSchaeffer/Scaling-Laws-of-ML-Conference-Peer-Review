**Verdict.** Both committees saw the same strengths and the same weaknesses. The method is simple and clearly written, and it beats the human-data baseline (BCP) with real humans. Against that stand modest novelty, one environment and no theory. No reviewer found a flaw in the method or the experiments. The gap came from the reviewer draw and from how each AC settled novelty. A drew one thin champion and two 5s; its AC broke the tie with an emergency reviewer whose input is not on the record. B drew three accept votes, two of them careful; its AC overruled the lone novelty objection on its own reading. A did hold one fact that B never weighed: Tylkin et al. also trained helpers against partly trained checkpoints. The submission cites that work twice ([62, 63]) but never discusses the overlap, and the authors conceded that FCP extends it.

**Committee A (reject).** Reviewer_TwiA's 8 is the only accept vote. It rests on one hour, four sentences of praise and no reply to the rebuttal. Reviewer_xzpB's 5 rests on generic points: no theory and one environment. xzpB also claimed a "similar idea" in computer vision without a reference, then ignored two author requests to explain it. Reviewer_tdpf (5) made the specific case. tdpf named Tylkin et al. and the uncited PLASTIC. tdpf also caught an overclaim: the Section 5.2 heading says FCP achieves "the highest scores across all maps", but tdpf found near-ties on two maps in the appendix. The authors conceded all three points. tdpf kept the 5, called many seeds and skill levels "a small change", and asked why FCP works. Yet tdpf wrote "I would have voted a clear accept but for the two issues" and was "not opposed to acceptance". The AC located the Tylkin citations and asked for a past-checkpoints-only baseline. It then cited an unseen emergency reviewer who "shared the same concerns", and rejected.

**Committee B (spotlight).** Reviewer_5uRi's 9 is thin: two hours, four sentences of praise, three questions and "Yes." for limitations. The case rests on Reviewer_sJ2R (8, ten hours) and Reviewer_6sVb (7). sJ2R also noted one environment and no theory, but knew of no theory that could apply. 6sVb wrote the most searching critique in either committee. Without human data, FCP is biased away from human-like partners, and its gains may reflect general robustness rather than convention matching. 6sVb accepted once the authors agreed to state these limits. One 6sVb claim is wrong: Knott et al. is cited, as [35]. Reviewer_bYPA (5, eleven hours) argued that FCP is domain randomization with the co-player as the environment. The argument is principled but concerns framing. bYPA conceded that no prior work applies domain randomization to human co-play, and rose from 4 to 5. The AC rejected the objection in its own words and chose a spotlight.

**Issues one committee raised and the other did not.** Only A raised the Tylkin overlap, PLASTIC, the per-map overclaim, the lack of a diversity measure for partners and the missing baseline. Only B raised domain randomization, held-out agents that resemble the training pool, human-likeness bias, the robustness-versus-convention confound and checkpoint hyperparameters. Both raised modest novelty and one environment. A also stressed the lack of theory, which sJ2R in B discounted. Most of this is weighting, not error. The Tylkin overlap is the one A issue with real weight, and it bears on the degree of novelty. B's AC called the details novel "as far as I know", and sJ2R said they could not judge the human-agent literature. So B never weighed that point.
