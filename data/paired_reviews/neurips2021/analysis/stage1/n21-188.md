**Verdict.** Both committees saw the same facts and weighted them differently. Ratings were almost the same: A gave 5, 7, 6, 4 (mean 5.5), B gave 6, 6, 7, 4 (mean 5.75). Every reviewer who listed a weakness named the synthetic, templated language or the toy 2D world. Nobody found a technical error. In A, the rebuttal moved nobody toward acceptance, and no reviewer argued for the paper afterwards. A's area chair (AC) added its own reason: TFT is "almost at 100% F1", so the task may be too easy. Figure 4 contradicts this. TFT scores about 0.77, 0.79 and 0.22 on systematic splits 3 to 5. In B, the rebuttal landed. Reviewer_viF6 raised to 6, and Reviewer_8BQz got the hard-negative tests it asked for. B's AC read the unedited 4 of Reviewer_MYUM as "5+" from discussion and called the toy language acceptable for a testbed. The split is a different tolerance for a synthetic benchmark. A's post-rebuttal comments and AC held it against the paper; B's did not.

**Committee A (reject).** Confidences 4, 3, 4, 4. Reviewer_P8h7 wrote the most specific negative review, and its checks hold. The paper says prior meanings "rarely consider spatial reference to objects" (lines 307-308) but cites neither Touchdown, Room2Room nor ALFRED. Figure 1's "Natural English version" is never used, and no single-modality baseline exists. After the rebuttal P8h7 stayed "leaning towards rejection" and asked for an RL test of the truth function. Reviewer_3aA5 correctly noted that each systematic split withholds only one to three word combinations (lines 255-270). It first called the motivation "quite clear". After reading the other reviews, it kept 6 but called the task's utility unclear. Reviewer_xTkz is weak. Its limitations section repeats its main review. It credits generalization to "the Transformer", but SFT transformers score at or below the LSTM baselines (lines 221-223). It never replied. Reviewer_Zjb3's 7 lists generic strengths and one correct weakness, near-ceiling scores, and it never engaged. The AC followed the post-rebuttal comments, not the two-two split. It credited "one of the reviewers" with suggesting ALFRED data; 3aA5 had suggested Room2Room as a "(Minor)" check.

**Committee B (poster).** Confidences 3, 2, 3, 3, lower than A's. 8BQz asked for hard negatives. The authors reported a drop under 5% for hard trajectories and about 0.3 on splits 1-2 for shuffled words. 8BQz said it was "happy to raise the score" but never edited its review. viF6 found the writing hard, got answers on negatives, animal motion and contributions, and raised to 6. Reviewer_3pgx's 7 restates the abstract and lists no weakness. It carries no argument. MYUM's core objection, a toy language and an "obvious" result, is a judgement A shared. Two side points are wrong. One-hot words pass through a learned projection (lines 149, 171-172), and the paper cites compositional grounding work ([22], [26], [34], [37], [40]). The AC leaned on the post-discussion consensus.

**Issues one committee raised and the other did not.** Only A raised positioning against ALFRED, Room2Room, Touchdown and CLEVRER, an RL use of the truth function, vocabulary-size and split-size ablations, real-language tests, near-ceiling scores and missing single-modality baselines. The positioning point is correct: even the camera-ready omits ALFRED, Room2Room and Touchdown. No rebuttal experiment could answer it. Only B raised the obviousness of the object-identity result, classification against description generation, the one-hot encoding and the Tenbrink literature. Both raised the synthetic language, shortcut learning, missing data-generation details and unclear notation. The decisive issue, the synthetic benchmark, was shared. Only its weight differed.
