**Verdict.** The committees weighed the same contribution differently, and A's reviewers tested the experiments harder. Both saw a known key-value architecture with new three-factor plasticity rules, tested only on synthetic tasks. In B, Reviewer_3w4j (10 hours) showed that the architecture is a one-step modern Hopfield network. 3w4j still judged the rules novel and enough for acceptance. The authors conceded, and all three B reviewers raised their scores. A's reviewers attacked the comparison with Hopfield networks, and the submission confirms their points. The cues zero 60% of the entries instead of flipping them (line 154); the authors called this a "flaw". No figure has error bars (checklist 3(c): No). Random KEVIN, the more plausible variant, matches Hopfield capacity (0.161N against 0.143N) with twice the synapses (Fig. 2b, line 170). On the auto- and heteroassociative benchmarks, only sequential KEVIN clearly wins, and A judged its slot cycling biologically implausible. BAM beats KEVIN on copy-paste (lines 275-276). Random KEVIN does beat Hopfield on continual and correlated recall (Fig. 4). The authors sent the same reframing to both committees. It answered B's main objection, not A's. No fatal flaw separates the committees.

**Committee A (reject).** Ratings 6, 5, 5, 6; mean 5.5; no champion. Reviewer_Dwry rose from 4 to 6 after the authors conceded that the sequential gate gives KEVIN information that Hopfield lacks. Reviewer_vmVW (20 hours) rose from 4 to 5. The reframing settled vmVW's placement concern, but vmVW stayed "not convinced about the significance of the experimental results". Reviewer_dV7b (confidence 5) kept 6 and called the idea "extremely obvious". Reviewer_dTBY (5) found the method incremental and credited the gains to sequential writing and task-specific additions. Fig. 2a supports dTBY: Hopfield is more accurate than random KEVIN beyond about 30 stimuli. dTBY never answered the rebuttal. The area chair (AC) rejected on significance. The AC also cited two points that no reviewer made a condition. The first, an incomplete comparison with Kanerva's sparse distributed memory, is weak. Section 2 cites Kanerva at lines 48, 69, 72 and 79, and itself states the step-function versus softmax difference that the AC raises. The second, no fit to biological data, is correct; lines 293-294 concede it.

**Committee B (poster).** Ratings 6, 7, 7; mean 6.67. 3w4j's 7 is principled. The review locates the novelty exactly, and the authors met each condition by concession and rewording. Reviewer_Fi9m's 7 is weaker. Fi9m wrote that naturalistic tasks and memory-network baselines "would be necessary" to show general interest. The rebuttal declined both, yet Fi9m raised the score. Fi9m also wrote that the model performs "similar or better than Hopfield networks and other memory networks"; Fig. 2a and Fig. 5c contradict this. Reviewer_Y8iA's review is short and generic. Y8iA answered only after an author reminder, in three short sentences, and moved from 5 to 6. The AC summarised the consensus and called the simulations "convincing". No B reviewer examined the experimental controls.

**Issues one committee raised and the other did not.** Only A questioned the fairness of the Hopfield comparison: zeroed cues, the sequential gate, twice the synapses and no error bars. Only A raised the exponential capacity of modern Hopfield networks and the weak copy-paste result (vmVW), ADAM and RNN baselines (dTBY), and reservoir and pruning references (Dwry). Only B stated the exact identity with a one-step modern Hopfield network and rejected the feedforward-versus-attractor contrast (3w4j). Only B asked about H-Mem, the undefined value learning rate and the weight initialization (Fi9m). Both raised synthetic-only tasks, missing machine-learning baselines, and Hopfield's higher accuracy at high load.
