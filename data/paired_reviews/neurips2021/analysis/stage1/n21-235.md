**Verdict.** The two area chairs (ACs), not the reviewers, made the split. They weighted the same facts in opposite directions. Both panels saw a clearly written first benchmark for soft-robot co-design. It is simplified on purpose (2D voxels, mass-spring physics), and no baseline solves its hard tasks. B's panel was the more positive: mean 6.33 against 6.0, and two of three ratings above threshold against two of four. The rebuttal raised a B score (Reviewer_rqAv, 5 to 6) and lowered an A score (Reviewer_j4pt, 5 to 4). Both ACs then wrote nearly the same judgment: useful to the learning community, of little use for real soft robots. A's AC accepted on the first half; B's AC rejected on the second. Neither AC addressed the one confirmed, checkable flaw that moved a score: Figure 3 averages only three seeds.

**Committee A (poster).** Ratings 7, 4, 8, 5. Reviewer_nQ7Q (8) wrote the most thorough review and commented twice. nQ7Q kept 8 after the authors promised API documentation and tutorials for extending the benchmark. Reviewer_VGKK's 7 carries little weight: one hour, confidence 3, and a request for code that the abstract links. Reviewer_j4pt (4) made checkable points that the paper confirms: three seeds, "optimal" language, noise-free sensing, fixed objects. The offer of six seeds did not satisfy j4pt, who called it a low standard "for a benchmark paper" and lowered the score. Reviewer_523c (5, confidence 5) wanted 3D, rotational motors and rigid-only robots; the last two lie outside a soft-robot benchmark. 523c never answered the authors' scaling table. The AC's case is its own: the OpenAI Gym analogy, a bridge to evolutionary computation, and access without large compute. The record strains the last point. Runs take several to twenty hours on 80-processor machines (lines 273-277), and the authors blamed "huge computation cost" for using three seeds.

**Committee B (reject).** Ratings 5, 8, 6. Reviewer_t5kh's 8 at confidence 5 rests on three short questions and "I do not have any serious objections", with no discussion. It is weak evidence, and the AC did not mention it. Reviewer_1ku4 (5) called the benchmark "a very valuable contribution". 1ku4 kept 5 because the paper spends its space on known algorithms instead of showing the framework's strengths. Reviewer_rqAv (6) questioned the voxel representation, the simple physics and the reality gap. rqAv then accepted the reply: modular robotics "justifies voxels enough", and simple physics matches what current algorithms can handle. The AC's three reasons are rqAv's pre-rebuttal points, and the meta-review ignores the rebuttal. The concerns have textual support. The paper twice calls voxels a "general and universal representation" (lines 46-47, 135-136). Section 6 names no limitation of the 2D physics or the reality gap.

**Issues one committee raised and the other did not.** Only A raised the three seeds, noise-free sensing and fixed object shapes (j4pt). Only A raised the missing societal impact (nQ7Q) and scaling beyond about 100 voxels (523c). Only B noted that the simulator resembles Medvet et al. 2020 (2D-VSR-Sim), which the paper cites but never compares. The authors conceded it is "similar to ours" with "comparable speeds" (rqAv). Only B argued that experiments on known algorithms crowd out evidence for the framework (1ku4). Both panels asked how fast the simulator is, and the submission reports no simulation speed. The authors told B it is slower than MuJoCo.
